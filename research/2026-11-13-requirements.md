# What the sources say about requirements and their verification (2026-11-13)

Primary-source check of the rules in the draft skills `writing-requirements`, `writing-proofs`, `requirements` and the `review-requirements` agent, written before those drafts are revised. Labels: **VERIFIED** = I read it in a primary source (source and section or page given). **VERIFIED-secondary** = only a summary, third-party paper or vendor page was reachable (named). **INFERRED** = my reasoning, or from memory, for datum. Page numbers for the NASA handbook are the printed ones; for the STPA handbook they are the PDF page, which equals the printed page.

## Summary: the findings that matter for the skills

1. **The draft's EARS table is right on the five basic patterns and wrong on the sixth.** EARS calls the combined pattern "complex", not "compound". The draft defines it as "two of the above together" and shows only "While…, when…". EARS also lets `If…then` carry preconditions: "While the aircraft is in-flight, if reverse thrust is commanded, then the control system shall inhibit…". That is the form a hazard state plus an unwanted trigger needs, and the draft does not show it (Q2).
2. **EARS is a syntax, not a quality check.** Its own authors state it was built and tested on high-level stakeholder requirements, makes no claim for all levels, and did not address conflicts or traceability (Mavin et al. 2009, §2, §6). The EARS ruleset allows one or many responses per "shall"; datum's one-"shall" rule is stricter and is the right call (GtWR C5 and NASA agree). EARS cannot replace the GtWR rules (Q1, Q2).
3. **The draft is missing several rules that all sources share.** NASA Appendix C and GtWR (rule names verified) both add: banned vague and unverifiable words (a long list), escape clauses ("as appropriate"), open-ended clauses ("etc."), pronouns, defined terms used consistently, and explicit tolerances. They also add four checks the draft lacks: is this requirement necessary ("what is the worst that could happen if it were left out?"), is it at the right level, is it feasible, and is the tolerance too tight ("what if it were doubled or tripled?") (Q1).
4. **NASA prefers positive statements; STPA writes constraints as "must not".** NASA Appendix C C.3 says state requirements positively, not "shall not". GtWR restricts "not" (R16). STPA constraints are inverted hazards, many of them prohibitions. The draft's own example (REQ-9C2T, "shall propose no session…") is a negative. The skill needs a rule for when a prohibition is the right form (Q1, Q4).
5. **STPA gives an exact form for hazard-answering requirements, and the draft does not use it.** A system-level constraint is `<System> & <Condition to Enforce> & <Link to Hazards>`, got by inverting the hazard. For when the hazard does occur there is a second form: "If <hazard> occurs, then <what needs to be done to prevent or minimize a loss>". The handbook says constraints must not name a solution, and its own examples carry `TBD` values. The draft's rule 2 covers both ideas in one sentence but gives neither form. The second form maps directly onto EARS `unwanted` (Q4).
6. **Choosing a method: the sources give a confidence order and a cost tie-break, not a default.** ECSS-E-ST-10-02C lists test, analysis, review-of-design, inspection "in order of precedence" for confidence. Its handbook adds: if several methods give the same confidence, pick the one with least effort; avoid over-testing and duplication across levels; every safety-critical function is verified by test; a requirement verified only by analysis or design review gets a risk assessment. The draft's "enough confidence at least cost" is this tie-break. It lacks the safety-critical rule and the analysis-only risk check (Q5).
7. **Datum's method definitions differ from NASA's and ECSS's in two places.** NASA's demonstration is a basic confirmation without detailed data gathering, not "a person judges what no proxy can measure". ECSS folds demonstration into test and has a fourth method, review-of-design, that covers examining approved records. Datum's "test = checked by a program" is its own software addition. These are defensible, but they are datum definitions and the skill should say so (Q5).
8. **A pass criterion has a precedent as a separate column.** NASA's verification matrix keeps "verification success criteria" apart from the "shall" text, and its procedures call for "values with tolerances for acceptance or rejection". ECSS analysis must show the specified performance "with a defined margin" and cover nominal and worst-case conditions. STPA's worst-case-environment stance means a hazard proof should say which worst-case conditions it uses. No source gives sample sizes for statistical criteria; Gilb's Meter is the nearest ("at least 20 representative cases") (Q5).
9. **The draft's "no invented values" rule is stricter than NASA's, deliberately.** NASA (App C.3) prefers a best-estimate value marked TBR, with rationale, owner and date to eliminate it. STPA's own examples use bare `TBD`. Datum's rule (no value until settled; each TBD gets an issue) is a position for AI authors, who fill gaps with plausible numbers; it needs a stated reason in the notes (Q1, Q4).
10. **The reviewer misses several checks the sources list.** NASA SEH 4.2.1.2.4 gives six questions (written correctly, technically correct, satisfy stakeholders, feasible, verifiable, redundant or over-specified). Missing from `review-requirements`: feasibility, redundancy, level, terminology against the glossary, unverifiable words, whether assumptions are stated. NASA SRR criteria also want a preliminary verification method for each requirement, which datum already does (Q6).
11. **Datum's own past lessons point the same way as the sources:** a verification programme larger than the build (L4), a budget verified by an analysis that never existed (L6), about 100 safety requirements answering risks scored by guesses (L7). They support "enough confidence at least cost", measure-before-value, and the closing review (Q7).

## 1. Writing a requirement

**Individual requirement.** The GtWR v4 lists nine characteristics for a requirement: necessary, appropriate, unambiguous, complete, singular, feasible, verifiable, correct, conforming (C1 to C9). VERIFIED-secondary: names from Wheaton and Herber (arXiv 2410.21288, Table 3, and 2401.16330, Table 1); one-line meanings for C1 to C7 from the Jama summary: necessary = defines a capability, characteristic, constraint or quality factor that is required; appropriate = detail matches the level of abstraction; unambiguous = every audience reads it one way; complete = stands on its own; singular = exactly one capability or constraint; feasible = realisable within cost, schedule and technical limits at acceptable risk; verifiable = structured so its realisation can be confirmed. The 29148 list is the same nine (VERIFIED-secondary, a research paper and a vendor page; 29148 itself unread).

**Set.** GtWR C10 to C15: complete, consistent, feasible, comprehensible, able to be validated, correct (VERIFIED-secondary, same papers). 29148 gives a similar set of four to five (Wikipedia, VERIFIED-secondary).

**Rules.** GtWR v4 has 42 rules, R1 to R42 (names VERIFIED-secondary, arXiv 2401.16330 §2.3): structured statements, active voice, appropriate subject-verb, defined terms, definite articles, common units, vague terms, escape clauses, open-ended clauses, superfluous infinitives, separate clauses, grammar, spelling, correct condition, logical expressions, use of "not", oblique symbol, single-thought sentence, combinators, purpose phrases, parentheses, enumeration, supporting diagram or model, pronouns, headings, absolutes, explicit conditions, multiple conditions, classification, unique expression, solution free, universal qualification, range of values, measurable performance, temporal dependencies, consistent terms and units, acronyms, abbreviations, style guide, decimal format, related needs and requirements, structured sets. The Jama page gives wording for a few: vague quantifiers and adjectives are banned (R7); escape clauses such as "where possible" and "as appropriate" fail (R8); "not" is restricted but "shall not" remains permissible (R16); combinators are flagged (R19); implementation is limited to cases with documented rationale (R31); measurable targets replace "fast" (R34). Everything else about what each rule says is INFERRED from its name.

**NASA, the primary text I could read.**
- A requirement is a complete sentence with a single "shall", a rationale, and key driving requirements (those with large cost or schedule effect) identified (SEH 4.2.1.2.3, p.57).
- NPR 7123.1B C.1.2.4e (p.51): individually clear, correct, feasible; not stated as how to satisfy; one interpretation; one actor-verb-object; validatable at the level stated. In pairs or as a set: no redundancy, consistent terms, not in conflict, a set of "design-to" requirements.
- SEH Appendix C (pp.197-200), the checklist: shall, will, should have fixed meanings (shall = requirement, will = fact, should = goal); active voice; "The product shall…" form; free of implementation ("ask why you need the requirement"); free of operations, so "The operator shall…" is almost always an operational statement; stated positively, not "shall not"; minimise TBD and prefer a best estimate marked TBR with rationale, owner and date; an intelligible rationale including assumptions, which must be confirmed before baselining; no indefinite pronouns, "as appropriate", "etc.", "and/or", "but not limited to"; one thought per statement; tolerances on performance values; "Are all requirements needed? If it is not necessary, it is not a requirement. Ask: what is the worst that could happen if it was not included?"; "What is the worst that could happen if the tolerance was doubled or tripled?"; error detection, handling and recovery, and "undesired events… and their required responses specified"; a list of unverifiable terms (flexible, easy, sufficient, safe, adequate, user-friendly, usable, fast, robust, quickly, easily, other "ly" and "ize" words, and others); requirements weakly coupled so a change ripples little.
- EARS paper §2 lists the eight problems it targets: ambiguity, vagueness, complexity, omission (especially of unwanted behaviour), duplication, wordiness, inappropriate implementation, untestability. It says conflict and traceability were out of scope.

**Which rules matter most for an AI agent (INFERRED).** An agent errs by being plausible and fluent, not by being careless. So the rules that bite are: (a) invented numbers and invented requirements (necessity, "worst thing if left out", no unsettled values); (b) vague and unverifiable words, which an agent produces freely; (c) bundled requirements ("and"), which fluent text hides; (d) solution words slipping in; (e) implicit context in a statement that is meant to stand alone (pronouns, undefined terms), because a different agent later writes cases from it; (f) universal claims ("always", "all", "never") that no proof can cover. Rationale and assumptions matter for the same reason: the agent that writes cases never saw the conversation.

**Implications for datum.**
- *Right:* one "shall"; verifiable; what not how; no invented values; the table of non-requirements ("shall be fast", "shall handle errors"); rationale with a measure (matches NASA, Volere, Gilb).
- *Missing:* escape and open-ended clauses (R8, R9), pronouns and defined terms (R4, R24, R36; datum already has a glossary), absolutes and universal quantifiers (R26, R32), explicit conditions (R27), tolerances. Missing also: necessity ("what is the worst that could happen if it is left out"), level (C2), feasibility (C6), and a statement that it must stand alone (C4). Name these in the rules list of `writing-requirements`, perhaps as a short list of words to avoid, citing NASA Appendix C.
- *Conflict:* "shall not" with nothing that could make it do X is called overbuilding in the draft, but NASA's positive-form rule and the draft's own REQ-9C2T pull in different directions. Add a line: a prohibition is the right form when the hazard is an action the part must never take; otherwise state the positive response.
- *Position to document:* "no invented values" is stricter than NASA's best-estimate-plus-TBR. Record the reason in `docs/design-notes.md` (an agent supplies plausible values; a TBR's estimate would be read as agreed).
- *Rationale:* NASA wants one on every requirement and wants assumptions in it (C.3). Datum requires it only with a measure or a chosen response (Fields). That is a narrower position; keep it but know it differs.

## 2. EARS

**The patterns, from the primary text.** VERIFIED (Mavin et al. 2009 §4; official guide, current).
- Generic: `While <optional pre-condition>, when <optional trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>`. Ruleset (official guide): zero or many preconditions, zero or one trigger, one system name, one or many responses. Clauses always in this order; the order follows temporal logic: preconditions first, then the trigger, then the response "if and only if" both hold (2009 §4.1).
- Ubiquitous: `The <system> shall <response>`; always active, no keyword.
- Event-driven: `When <trigger>, the <system> shall <response>`. In the 2009 paper the trigger is an event "detected at the system boundary".
- State-driven: `While <precondition(s)>, the <system> shall <response>`. The 2009 paper allows `During` as a readability variant.
- Optional feature: `Where <feature is included>, the <system> shall <response>`.
- Unwanted behaviour: `If <trigger>, then the <system> shall <response>`. In the 2009 paper: `IF <optional preconditions> <trigger>, THEN…`. "Unwanted behaviour" is a general term "to cover all situations that are undesirable", including failures, disturbances, deviations from desired user behaviour, and unexpected behaviour of interacting systems (§4.4). The response "mitigates the impact of the unwanted event, or prevents the system from entering an unwanted state". The authors say unwanted behaviour "is a major source of omissions in early requirements", which is why it has its own syntax. Footnote 2 says the line between wanted and unwanted is "a matter of viewpoint, or even a matter of style" (a system with redundancy behaves normally when a part fails).
- Complex: "requirements that include more than one EARS keyword" (official guide); e.g. `While <precondition>, when <trigger>, the <system> shall…`. The 2009 paper (§4.7) also combines `While`, `When` and `Where` inside `If…then`: "While the aircraft is in-flight, if reverse thrust is commanded, then the control system shall inhibit thrust reverser deployment", and "When selecting idle setting, if aircraft data is unavailable, then…".
- Open point in the 2009 paper (§7): how `If` and `When` apply to unwanted *states* was not settled; "unwanted states may require an additional template" for behaviour while in an unwanted state.
- Scope claim: "most suitable to the definition of high-level stakeholder requirements"; no claim for all levels of decomposition (§2). Results: 36 raw requirements became 47, average words fell from 36.9 to 25.6; ambiguity, vagueness and wordiness were reduced, not removed (§5, §6). Single small case study, high-level safety requirements only (§6 limitations).

**Check against the draft.**
- Ubiquitous, event, state, optional, unwanted: syntax matches. The draft writes `unwanted` as "If <unwanted condition>, then…"; EARS says `<trigger>`, and the 2009 paper allows preconditions.
- `compound` is datum's name for EARS "complex". The definition "Two of the above together" is narrower than EARS (more than one keyword, including `If…then` with `While`, `When` or `Where` inside) and the example list omits the unwanted combination.
- A hazard state or a failure as a trigger fits `If…then`; a hazard *state* the part may sit in for a while fits `While <state>, …`. The second is the case the 2009 paper flagged as unsettled; the draft already uses `state` for it, which is a reasonable reading (INFERRED).
- The draft's one-"shall" rule is stricter than EARS (one or many responses). Keep it; GtWR C5 and NASA ("one actor-verb-object") support it.
- EARS says nothing on measures, units or values, on conflicts, or on traceability. Those must come from elsewhere (GtWR, NASA).

**Implications for datum.** Rename or annotate `compound` as "EARS complex" and extend the line to say any combination of `While`, `When`, `Where` and `If…then`, in EARS order. Add the example "While <state>, if <trigger>, then…" for hazards. State that the trigger of an `event` requirement is something observable at the part's boundary, which matches the draft's "what is observed from outside" idea (INFERRED). Do not present EARS as a full quality rule set; the draft does not, but the validator's "one `shall`" warning plus the pattern field covers only syntax.

## 3. Where requirements come from per function

**What the sources say.**
- NASA splits allocated requirements into functional ("what functions need to be performed"), performance ("how well these functions should be performed") and interface requirements, plus crosscutting ones (environmental, safety, human factors, the "-ilities", standards) (SEH 4.2.1.2.2, p.57). Worked example: "The TVC shall provide vehicle control…" becomes four measured requirements (maximum gimbal angle ±0.1 degree, rate, force, frequency response), each with a tolerance.
- NPR 7123.1B C.1.2.4(c), (d) (pp.50-51): define the functional and behavioural expectations, then "the performance requirements associated with each defined functional and behavioral expectation"; performance is "the quantitative part of a requirement to indicate how well each product function is expected to be accomplished", "any qualitative performance expectations should be analyzed and quantified", and those "that can be changed by tradeoff analysis should be identified". Constraints are "typically not able to be changed based on tradeoff analyses" (C.1.2.4b).
- NASA completeness and reliability checks (App C.4, pp.198-199): have any of functional, performance, interface, environment, facility, transportation, training, personnel, operability, safety, security, appearance and design requirements been overlooked; are performance specifications and margins listed (timing, throughput, storage size, latency, accuracy and precision); "Are there error detection, reporting, handling, and recovery requirements?"; "Are undesired events (e.g. single-event upset, data loss or scrambling, operator error) considered and their required responses specified?".
- Gilb, Planguage (tutorial slides pp.14-23, 31): quality and performance requirements are written on a defined **Scale** (the unit of measure) with a **Meter** (the practical way of measuring it) and levels: benchmarks (Past, Record, Trend), targets (Goal, Stretch, Wish) and constraints (Fail, Survival, Must: "a level needed to avoid a system failure of some kind"). His quality-control rules for a spec: performance requirements must be quantified; sources specified; unambiguous; clear enough to test; consistent with sources and siblings (p.31). His example Meter names a sample: "A formal test in field with at least 20 representative cases" (pp.20-23). The slides do not say how to find the functions from which to derive performance requirements.
- Volere (Robertson), Edition 13: every functional requirement "should have a fit criterion or a test case"; the fit criterion "is the benchmark to allow the tester to determine whether the implemented product has met the requirement"; constraints should each have a rationale and a fit criterion "as they help to expose false constraints (solutions masquerading as constraints)". I did not read the sentence in the template that says every requirement is measurable, only the vendor summary of it (VERIFIED-secondary).
- STPA adds a third source: sub-hazards by "what do we need to control to prevent this hazard" produce constraints on specific processes, with TBD values (Handbook pp.21-22, Table 2.1).

**What no source gave.** I found no established method that walks each function through a fixed list of questions. NASA does it as "function statement → functional requirement + performance requirement", then checks completeness against the list above. The draft's three questions (attained, how well, unwanted behaviour) map onto this as: attained = NASA functional requirement; how well = NASA performance requirement and Gilb Scale/Meter/levels; unwanted behaviour = the NASA reliability checks and EARS's unwanted pattern. INFERRED that the mapping is complete for a per-function pass.

**Implications for datum.**
- *Right:* the three questions have footing in NASA (functional, performance, undesired events) and EARS (the stated cause of omissions).
- *Position, not a source rule:* "How well: only where someone cares; do not invent one." NASA's checklist leans the other way (are all performance specifications and margins listed) but its own "what if the tolerance were doubled" check supports restraint, and the Pendentive audit (L4, L7) supports it. Keep it, and say it is a datum position.
- *Missing from the draft:* a check of the NASA list of overlooked areas (interfaces, environment, operability, security, safety, appearance) against the part, at least in the reviewer. Datum has interfaces in synthesis, so only the rest need naming (INFERRED).
- *Useful from Gilb:* Must/Fail versus Goal is a distinction the measure's bounds do not carry. A `bound` is a pass level; whether it is a minimum acceptable (Fail/Must) or a target (Goal) changes how hard a proof must try and whether the builder will trade it. NPR C.1.2.4(d) asks to mark tradeable values. Consider one optional field or a rationale convention; this is a possible gap, not a defect (INFERRED).
- *Useful from Gilb and NASA:* a benchmark (Gilb's Past) is a good source for a value; the draft's `running-a-spike` covers measuring one.
- *Key driving requirements* (NASA 4.2.1.2.3): none in the draft; useful for deciding where proof effort goes, tied to Q5 (INFERRED).

## 4. Hazards to requirements (STPA)

**The exact forms** (STPA Handbook, pp.17-21).
- Hazard: "a system state or set of conditions that, together with a particular set of worst-case environmental conditions, will lead to a loss" (p.17). Written as `<System> & <Unsafe Condition> & <Link to Losses>`, e.g. "H-1: Aircraft violate minimum separation standards in flight [L-1, L-2, L-4, L-5]" (p.19). Criteria (p.18): a state, not a component-level cause; leads to loss in some worst-case environment; a state to be prevented. Not "unsafe", "unintended", "accidental" (p.19-20 tips). About seven to ten hazards at system level is a rule of thumb (p.19).
- System-level constraint: "specifies system conditions or behaviors that need to be satisfied to prevent hazards (and ultimately prevent losses)" (p.20). "Simply invert the condition": `<System-level Constraint> = <System> & <Condition to Enforce> & <Link to Hazards>`; "SC-1: Aircraft must satisfy minimum separation standards from other aircraft and objects [H-1]"; "SC-2: Aircraft airframe integrity must be maintained under worst-case conditions [H-2]". Traceability need not be one-to-one: one constraint may prevent several hazards, several may serve one hazard.
- **When the hazard does occur.** "Constraints can also define how the system must minimize losses in case the hazards do occur." Form: `<System-level Constraint> = If <hazard> occurs, then <what needs to be done to prevent or minimize a loss> & <Link to Hazards>`; "SC-3: If aircraft violate minimum separation, then the violation must be detected and measures taken to prevent collision [H-1]" (p.20).
- Constraints "should not specify a particular solution or implementation": SC-3 says detect and act, not "install a collision avoidance system"; naming a solution is "premature at this early stage" (pp.20-21).
- Refinement: for each hazard ask what must be controlled; sub-hazards give specific constraints, which carry values as `TBD` (e.g. "Deceleration must occur within TBD seconds of landing… at a rate of at least TBD m/s2") (Table 2.1, pp.21-22).
- Later steps: each unsafe control action is inverted into a controller constraint (Table 2.5, p.41, e.g. "C-2: BSCU Autobrake must not provide Brake control action during a normal takeoff [UCA-2]"); scenarios then show how constraints can be violated and give further requirements (p.15, p.52). The traceability chain is losses, hazards, constraints, UCAs, controller constraints, scenarios (Figure 2.21, p.37).
- **Worst case, no excuse for safeguards.** STPA analyses the worst case: "we cannot omit UCAs when a safeguard exists" and the aim is to prevent the unsafe behaviour "even if safeguards do exist" (pp.37-38). So a proof of a constraint should not rely on a backup.
- A hazard that occurs is a signal: "Any occurrence of one of these hazards (even if an accident does not result) should trigger a complete review of the safety engineering process" (p.109). Not a requirement form, but it supports a leading-indicator check.

**How to write a requirement that "answers" a hazard (INFERRED, from the forms above).** Two kinds, matching STPA. (1) Prevent: the inverted condition, as an EARS `ubiquitous`, `state` or `unwanted` requirement on the part's behaviour, observable from outside; "must not" is acceptable when the hazard is a prohibited action. (2) Respond: `If <hazard state> occurs, then…` = EARS `unwanted`: detect it, and act to prevent or reduce the loss. In both, name the part and the condition, not a mechanism, link `answers: [HAZ]`, and keep the value `TBD` until settled. **To check it:** invert the requirement; the failure of the requirement, in the worst-case environment, should be the hazard state (the inversion test); the proof puts the part in that state under worst-case conditions and shows the loss does not follow; a requirement that does not constrain the hazard state, or that the part can satisfy while the hazard holds, fails the check.

**Implications for datum.**
- *Right:* hazards as states of the part and not causes (`losses-and-hazards`, matches pp.18-19); `answers` as a list (matches many-to-many); the reviewer's "hazard's state can no longer lead to its loss while the requirement holds" (matches the idea of inversion); proof "puts the part in the hazard's state" (matches the worst-case idea).
- *Missing:* the two forms. `writing-requirements` rule 2 ("what must hold so that the hazard's state cannot lead to its loss, or so the part does not enter that state") should name them: the inverted condition, and "If <hazard> occurs, then…". Add the "no solution" warning and the "worst-case environment" wording to `writing-proofs`' `conditions` guidance for hazard proofs.
- *Missing:* the draft never says a hazard-answering requirement can be about the response after the state is entered; rule 2's "or so the part does not enter" covers only prevention. The mitigation form is half of the handbook's constraint section.
- *Tension:* "must not" prohibitions versus NASA's positive-form rule (Q1). A short sentence resolves it.
- *Scope note:* STPA's step-1 constraints are system level; datum keeps them at the level of the part whose function introduces the hazard. That is a datum decision, not an STPA rule (Risk in the engine), and the hazard-count guidance (7 to 10) was written for a whole system.

## 5. Choosing a verification method

**Definitions.**
- NASA SEH 5.3, "Methods of verification" (p.93), VERIFIED:
  - *Analysis*: "mathematical modeling and analytical techniques to predict the suitability of a design… based on calculated data or data derived from lower system structure end product verifications"; "generally used when a prototype… is not available"; includes modelling and simulation and "verification by similarity of a heritage product".
  - *Demonstration*: "Showing that the use of an end product achieves the individual specified requirement. It is generally a basic confirmation of performance capability, differentiated from testing by the lack of detailed data gathering."
  - *Inspection*: "The visual examination of a realized end product", "generally used to verify physical design features or specific manufacturer identification"; "can include inspection of drawings, documents, or other records".
  - *Test*: "The use of an end product to obtain detailed data needed to verify performance"; "produces data at discrete points for each specified requirement under controlled conditions and is the most resource-intensive verification technique."
- ECSS-E-ST-10-02C 5.2.2 (Rev.1, 2018), VERIFIED: four methods, test (including demonstration), analysis (including similarity), review-of-design, inspection. *Test*: "measuring product performance and functions under representative simulated environments". *Analysis*: "theoretical or empirical evaluation using techniques agreed with the customer", including "systematic, statistical and qualitative design analysis, modelling and computational simulation". *Review-of-design*: "approved records or evidence that unambiguously show that the requirement is met". *Inspection*: "visual determination of physical characteristics", including "software source code conformance with coding standards".

**How to choose.**
- NASA SEH 5.3.1.2 (pp.90-92) gives factors, not a rule: project type, cost and schedule ("verification activities can be significant drivers of a project's cost and schedule… trade studies should be performed early"), risk (a trade "between performing a model test versus determining model characteristics by a less costly but less revealing analysis"; the team decides "what risks are acceptable in terms of the project's cost and schedule"), facilities, trained users, acquisition strategy, and "degree of design heritage and hardware/software reuse". The method is recorded with the requirement, and "should be determined as the requirements are developed" (Table 4.2-2, p.59). NPR 7123.1B Table G-4 (p.109) makes a "preliminary verification and validation method identified for each requirement" an SRR product.
- ECSS-E-ST-10-02C 5.2.1: the approach is set by analysing the requirements with design constraints, maturity of tools, "cost and schedule" and others. 5.2.2.1: the four methods are listed "in order of precedence that, in general, provides more confidence in the results".
- ECSS-E-HB-10-02A guidance on 5.2.2 (INFERRED section from the handbook structure; text VERIFIED): "Test is the preferred verification method as it provides generally a higher confidence"; "If several verification methods are possible with the same level of confidence on the verification results, the selection is oriented to minimize the required effort (impact on cost and schedule)"; "Analysis is used when flight conditions cannot be accurately simulated… and/or when it is not economically feasible to test the entire spectrum of flight conditions. Analysis and test methods are very often complementary"; "Duplication of verification activities on different levels should be avoided"; "Attention should be paid to avoid over-testing"; "Verification of critical requirements… should be achieved as soon as possible (e.g. at the lowest suitable level)"; test and analysis can be combined (lower-level test plus higher-level analysis) to avoid costly system-level tests; "Tests are selected based on their effectiveness in verifying the specified requirements considering lessons learned, statistical data from past projects"; the method must not risk personnel or hardware.
- **Normative rules** (ECSS-E-ST-10-02C 5.2.2.1, as reproduced in the handbook): "All safety critical functions shall be verified by test"; software verification "shall include testing in the target hardware environment"; "for each requirement verified only by analysis or review-of-design, a risk assessment… shall be conducted to determine the level (major/minor) of the impact"; if major, a mitigation plan that includes "a cross check based on two independent analyses". (The 2018 Word file I read dropped some of these list items in extraction; the handbook reproduces them.)
- Analysis must state assumptions and boundary conditions, "covers both the nominal and the worst case conditions", and take "analytical uncertainty… into account such that specified performance is demonstrated with a defined margin" (handbook guidance on 5.2.2.3).

**Pass criterion against the requirement's value.**
- NASA keeps them apart: Appendix D Table D-1 (p.202) has a "Shall statement" column and a separate "Verification success criteria" column. A verification procedure holds "characteristics and design criteria to be inspected, demonstrated or tested, including values with tolerances for acceptance or rejection" (Table 5.3-1, p.94). The requirement itself carries the value with its tolerance (App C.2, p.197).
- ECSS: the test report assesses results "in the light of the test requirements (including pass-fail criteria)" (DRD, test report); the verification control document explains "the verification close-out criteria" (5.4.4.1).
- Neither NASA nor ECSS (as read) gives sample sizes or confidence for statistical criteria; ECSS mentions "statistical" analysis only as a technique. Gilb's Meter is the nearest: it names the trials ("at least 20 representative cases") (tutorial pp.20-23).

**Implications for datum.**
- *Right:* "no default, rationale required, enough confidence at least cost, and why cheaper would not do": it is the ECSS tie-break (equal confidence, least effort) and NASA's cost-and-risk trade; the list of what to weigh (what it guards, cost at every release, what can change) reads as the same factors in software form. The `pass` that names the measure and not the number matches NASA's separate success-criteria column.
- *Missing:* (a) the safety-critical rule: hazard-answering requirements should be shown by test unless a reason is written; the draft says only that they "warrant more". (b) For `analysis`, state assumptions, boundary conditions and the worst case, and add a risk check when a requirement rests on analysis alone. (c) Avoid duplicate proofs at two levels, and prove at the lowest level that can show it (datum's Verification note says this already; the skill does not). (d) Say a proof must not itself endanger the user's data or machine (ECSS: method must not produce risks).
- *Datum definitions that differ from the sources,* to be stated as such: `demonstration` as "only a person can judge, no proxy" is not NASA's (a basic confirmation without detailed data); `test` as "checked by a program" is not NASA's or ECSS's; `inspection` also covers what ECSS calls review-of-design (records) and what NASA calls inspection of documents. The four names are shared, but not the boundaries. The design notes already call EASA codes a finer taxonomy; add a line that the boundaries are datum's.
- *Position to keep:* ECSS ranks test first for confidence, and datum rejects a default of `test`. The two are compatible: ECSS's ordering is about confidence and its tie-break is effort; datum's no-default rule is about forcing the rationale. Say so, so a reader does not take it for a contradiction.
- *Gap in the sources:* none found on sample size or confidence for a quality threshold; the `sample_min`, `confidence`, `bound` fields are datum's own (INFERRED from statistics, not from a standard).

## 6. Reviewing requirements

**NASA.**
- SEH 4.2.1.2.4 (pp.60-61): validate requirements in six questions. Written correctly (format and editorial errors); technically correct (a few trained reviewers first, checking bidirectional traceability, valid assumptions, essential to and consistent with the solution); satisfy stakeholders; feasible ("make technical sense and be possible to achieve"); verifiable ("stated… with enough information that it will be possible to verify"); redundant or over-specified ("unique… and necessary"). Validation results are "often a decisive factor" in proceeding; the team must be ready to show the set is complete and understandable.
- NPR 7123.1B 3.2.3.2 (p.17): validation ensures requirements are "well-formed (clear and unambiguous), complete (agrees with customer and stakeholder needs), consistent (conflict free), and individually verifiable and traceable to a higher level requirement or goal".
- SEH Appendix C.4 (pp.198-200) checklist, by heading: clarity, completeness, compliance (right level, no implementation, no operations, no personnel assignments), consistency ("without contradicting themselves or the requirements of related systems", terminology consistent with the glossary), traceability (needed, bidirectional, uniquely numbered), correctness (assumptions correct and confirmed), functionality ("necessary and together sufficient"), performance, interfaces, maintainability, reliability, verifiability, data usage ("don't care" conditions explicit).
- SRR (NPR 7123.1B Table G-4, pp.108-109): success criteria include "requirements… responsive to the parent requirements and represent achievable capabilities"; "maturity of the requirements definition… sufficient"; a sound process for allocating and controlling requirements; interfaces identified. Entrance products include a preliminary verification and validation method per requirement, a preliminary system safety analysis, and key driving requirements. The SEH, in its reproduction of the NPR requirements, lists "TBD/TBR closures" among requirement trends to measure (p.231).
- Conflicts: NASA treats them under consistency (above) and in the SRR; it gives no method for finding them beyond reading. Creep is a related management risk (SEH, requirements creep box, p.134), with techniques such as asking "What is the risk if the change is not approved?".

**GtWR.** VERIFIED-secondary only: the set characteristics C10 to C15 (complete, consistent, feasible, comprehensible, able to be validated, correct), and R41 "related needs and requirements" and R42 "structured sets" by name. The Jama page gives C11 as "unique, conflict-free" and C12 as the set being realisable within cost, schedule and technical limits. **29148:** not read. A vendor summary says its clause 6 has no activity for analysing requirements for conflicts and treats consistency only as a characteristic (VERIFIED-secondary, low reliability).

**EARS.** The 2009 authors hoped (not showed) that "applying the syntactic rules will clarify the precise preconditions and triggers, which should highlight conflicting requirements" (§7). A practical consequence (INFERRED): two requirements with the same trigger and state but different responses are a visible conflict, and EARS fields make that mechanical to find.

**Implications for datum.**
- *Right:* the reviewer starts from sources and looks for what has nothing attached (matches NASA's "necessary and together sufficient" and bidirectional traceability); it checks conflicts from the requirements alone; it separates the author from the reviewer, as NASA's "trained reviewers first, then stakeholders" does.
- *Missing checks, with the source:* feasibility (SEH step 4, GtWR C6); redundancy and duplicates (SEH step 6, NPR 3.2.3.2); right level (App C compliance, C2); unverifiable and vague words by list (App C verifiability); terminology against the glossary (App C consistency; datum has one); assumptions stated and confirmed (App C.3); tolerances too tight (App C performance); same trigger and state with different responses (EARS-based). Step 6 "overbuilding" covers only necessity.
- *Where to put them:* the first three are cheap to name in `review-requirements` step 3. Duplicate and same-trigger checks fit a script better than the reviewer, but the validator "judges structure", so for now the reviewer (INFERRED).
